Example: Analysis of Call Credit Vertical Spreads¶

In this example, we analyze call credit vertical spreads. These are bearish directional options strategies that involve selling a lower strike call and buying a higher strike call to limit upside risk.

Learning Objectives:

By the end of this example, you will be able to:

  • Construct profit diagrams for call vertical spreads: Build profit and loss profiles for call credit spreads by combining short and long call contracts with different strike prices, and compute the breakeven point where the position transitions from profit to loss.
  • Calculate probability of profit and probability of exercise for vertical spreads: Use cumulative distribution functions of projected share price distributions to determine the likelihood of profit at the breakeven point, and compute the probability that at least one leg will expire in-the-money.
  • Compute Delta for composite positions to assess hedging requirements: Calculate the net Delta for a call vertical spread by summing the weighted Deltas of individual legs, and determine whether the position is delta-neutral or requires hedging with underlying shares.

The build(...) method takes two arguments: the type to build, and a NamedTuple containing the strike price K, the premium paid for the option, the days to expiration DTE (units: years), the sense = 1 (long), the number of contracts in the copy field and the implied volatility in the IV field.

Task 1: Compute Profit, Loss and Breakeven Points¶

In this task, we compute the profit and loss (P&L) profile for the vertical credit spread position at expiration, as well as the breakeven points.

A call vertical spread is constructed by combining two call contracts: a short call contract generates income while the long call contract controls upside risk. This trade has a bearish outlook on the underlying asset.

Bearish call credit spread:

Let's build a bearish call credit spread for AMD whose current share price is $S(0) = 225.78$ USD/share. This trade structure is constructed by selling (short) a call with strike $K_{1}$ and buying (long) a call with strike $K_{2}$.

  • Leg 1: The strike price for the short call leg is given by $K_{1}$ = 230 USD/share with DTE = 58 days
  • Leg 2: The strike price for the long call leg is given by $K_{2}$ = 240 USD/share with DTE = 58 days

This trade will generate a net credit when opened. Depending on the share price at expiration, the trade can result in a profit or loss. The maximum profit occurs when the share price at expiration is below the lower strike price $K_{1}$. The maximum loss occurs when the share price at expiration is above the higher strike price $K_{2}$.

Profit and Breakeven Analysis for Call Vertical Spread¶

Let contract $j$ have a strike price of $K_{j}$ and premium $\mathcal{P}_{j}$. The share price at expiration is given by $S(T)$. Finally, let contract 1 be the short leg $\theta_{1} = -1$ and contract 2 be the long leg $\theta_{2} = 1$, where $K_{1} < K_{2}$ and $K_{1}$ is near the money. Then, the profit for a single call vertical spread at expiration is given by: $$ \hat{P}_{\mathcal{C}} = \left(S(T) - K_{2}\right)^{+} - \left(S(T) - K_{1}\right)^{+} + \left(\mathcal{P}_{1} - \mathcal{P}_{2}\right) $$

where $V_{c}(K,S(T)) = (S(T)-K)^{+}=\max(S(T)-K,0)$ is the payoff function for a call contract. The first term is the net payout of the two legs of the spread, while the second term is the net premium received (credit). The maximum possible profit, loss, and breakeven conditions are given by:

  • The maximum possible profit of $\left(\mathcal{P}_{1} - \mathcal{P}_{2}\right)$ will occur when $S(T)\leq{K_{1}}$ (both calls expire worthless).
  • The maximum possible loss of $K_{2} - K_{1} - \left(\mathcal{P}_{1} - \mathcal{P}_{2}\right)$ will occur when $S(T)\geq{K_{2}}$ (both calls are exercised).
  • The call vertical spread will breakeven when $S(T) = K_{1}+\left(\mathcal{P}_{1} - \mathcal{P}_{2}\right)$.

Let's visualize the profit and loss profile for the call vertical spread position.

Task 2: Compute the Probability of Profit (POP) and the Probability of Exercise (POE)¶

In this task, we compute the Probability of Profit (POP) and the Probability of Exercise (POE) for the call vertical spread position at expiration.

To start, we need to compute the distribution of future share prices at expiration using the SAGBM model parameters we loaded earlier. We use a Monte Carlo simulation to generate a large number of possible future share prices at expiration, and then compute the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the future share prices.

In [14]:
model = let

    # Initialize -
    ATM_IV = metadata["atm_IV"] |> x-> parse(Float64, x); # atm IV on purchase date, 10-22-2025

    # Case: drift = risk-free rate, volatility = max (atm_IV, historic volatility)
    model = build(MyGeometricBrownianMotionEquityModel, (
        μ = risk_free_rate, # let's mess with this ...
        σ = max(ATM_IV, filter(:ticker=> x-> x=="AMD", parameters_df)[!,:volatility] |> first)
    ));

    model;
end

MyGeometricBrownianMotionEquityModel(0.0431, 0.5903)

Now that we have a populated MyGeometricBrownianMotionEquityModel instance holding the AMD data, let's generate number_of_samples possible future share price values using the sample(...) function exported by the VLQuantitativeFinancePackage.jl package.

  • The sample method takes a model::MyGeometricBrownianMotionEquityModel instance as the first argument, along with the time values that we wish to sample: T₁::Float64 is the start time, T₂::Float64 is the stop time (in units of years), Δt::Float64 is the time step (in units of years), and Sₒ::Float64 denotes the initial share price (in units of USD/share). Finally, we pass in the number of samples we want to generate in the number_of_paths argument.
  • The sample method generates number_of_paths sample paths from $T_{1}\rightarrow{T}_{2}$ in steps of $\Delta{t}$. However, we only want the share prices corresponding to the duration of the contract, i.e., $T_{2} = \text{DTE}$. Thus, we pass the full samples array using the Julia pipe operator to a slicing operation where we select columns 2:end of the last row (col 1 holds the time). We store the number_of_paths samples of the share price at $T_{2}$ in the endpoint_samples::Array{Float64,1} variable.

So what do we get?

end;

Check: Are future GBM share prices log-normally distributed?¶

We expect geometric Brownian motion to generate log-normally distributed share prices. However, we can check this expectation by performing an exact one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test on the samples and a LogNormal distribution estimated using maximum-likelihood estimation.

Hypothesis: The H0:null hypothesis for the test is the data (endpoint samples generated using geometric Brownian motion) comes from the LogNormal distribution d. The alternative hypothesis H1:alternative is the samples are not drawn from the distribution d.

Let's start this check by fitting a LogNormal distribution exported by the Distributions.jl package using the fit_mle(...) function exported by Distributions.jl, and save this in the d_gbm::LogNormal variable:

In [16]:
d_gbm = fit_mle(LogNormal, endpoint_samples);

Next, execute the exact one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. We reject the H0:null hypothesis, i.e., the endpoint_samples samples are not drawn from d_gbm.

Alternative: Future share price projection using implied volatility¶

Alternatively, we could use the implied volatility to estimate the expected share price move over some time $0\rightarrow{T}$. Suppose we construct a Normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{IV},\sigma_{IV})$ where the mean $\mu_{IV} = S_{0}\exp(\bar{r}\cdot{T\Delta{t}})$, i.e., the risk-neutral future value of the current share price, or just $\mu_{IV}\approx{S}_{0}$ for short duration contracts, and the standard deviation $\sigma_{IV}$ is: $$ \sigma_{IV} = S_{0}\cdot\left(\frac{\text{IV}}{100}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\text{T}\cdot\Delta{t}} $$ using the Normal type exported by the Distributions.jl package. This is only an approximate model of the future share price; it has a couple of issues:

Watch out! This model assumes a Normal distribution, which is problematic because it can emit negative share prices. One way to fix this issue is to use a Truncated Normal distribution instead.

However, despite the potential issues, this model is widely used to generate quick estimates of future share price ranges using the forward-looking implied volatility. Let's save the Normal IV distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{IV},\sigma_{IV})$ in the d_IV::Normal variable:

In [19]:
d_IV = let

    # initialize -
    DTE = metadata["DTE"] |> x-> parse(Float64, x); # days to expiration (units: days)
    ATM_IV = metadata["atm_IV"] |> x-> parse(Float64, x); # atm IV on purchase date, 10-22-2025
    
    # build the Normal model -
    d = Normal(Sₒ*exp(risk_free_rate*DTE*Δt), Sₒ*(ATM_IV)*sqrt(DTE*Δt));
    d; # return

end

Probability of Profit (POP)¶

The POP for a call credit vertical spread position is given by the probability that the future share price at expiration $S(T)$ is less than or equal to the breakeven point.

Probability of Profit (POP) for Vertical Spread

The POP for a call vertical spread position is the probability that the future share price at expiration $S(T)$ is less than the breakeven point $B$: $$

\begin{align*} \text{POP} &= \mathbb{P}(S(T) \leq B) \\ & = F_{S}(B) \end{align*}

$$

where $F_{S}(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the future share price at expiration, i.e., $F_{S}(x) = \mathbb{P}(S(T) \leq x)$.

So what do we see?

What is the probability of exercise (POE) for the call vertical spread position?

The short call contract will be exercised if the share price at expiration is at least $\epsilon$ = 0.01 USD/share above the strike price of the short call contract, i.e., $S(T) > K_{1} + \epsilon$. The long call contract will be exercised if the share price at expiration is above the strike price of the long call contract, i.e., $S(T) > K_{2} + \epsilon$.

Probability of Exercise (POE) for Call Vertical Spread

Since $K_{1} < K_{2}$, if the long call is in-the-money, the short call is also in-the-money. Therefore, the overall POE for the call vertical spread position is determined by the short call at the lower strike: $$

\begin{align*} \text{POE} & = \mathbb{P}(S(T) > (K_{1} + \epsilon)) \\ & = 1 - F_{S}(K_{1}+\epsilon) \end{align*}

$$

where $F_{S}(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the future share price at expiration, i.e., $F_{S}(x) = \mathbb{P}(S(T) \leq x)$.

So what do we see?

What is the probability of maximum loss (POML) for the call vertical spread position?

Probability of Maximum Loss (POML)

The maximum loss for the call vertical spread position occurs when the share price at expiration $S(T)$ is at least $\epsilon$ = 0.01 USD/share above the higher strike price $K_{2}$. Therefore, the POML is determined by the long call strike $K_{2}$: $$

\begin{align*} \text{POE} & = \mathbb{P}(S(T) > (K_{2} + \epsilon)) \\ & = 1 - F_{S}(K_{2}+\epsilon) \end{align*}

$$

where $F_{S}(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the future share price at expiration, i.e., $F_{S}(x) = \mathbb{P}(S(T) \leq x)$.

Task 3: Is this trade Delta-neutral?¶

In this task, we determine if the call vertical spread position is Delta-neutral at the time of trade initiation. A position is considered Delta-neutral if the overall Delta of the position is zero.

The net Delta of a call vertical spread equals the sum of the individual Deltas of the short and long call contracts weighted by their position direction. A negative net Delta indicates the position benefits from a decrease in the underlying share price, while a positive net Delta indicates the position benefits from an increase in the share price.

Summary¶

This example demonstrates how to construct and analyze a call credit vertical spread position using profit diagrams, probability calculations, and Delta analysis.

Key Takeaways:

  • Call vertical spreads have defined risk and reward boundaries: The maximum profit equals the net credit received from selling the lower strike call and buying the higher strike call, occurring when the share price stays at or below the lower strike. The maximum loss equals the difference between strike prices minus the net credit, occurring when the share price rises above the higher strike.
  • Probability measures assess position risk from different perspectives: Probability of profit evaluates the likelihood that the share price remains at or below the breakeven point at expiration. Probability of exercise estimates the likelihood that the short leg will be assigned, requiring the trader to sell shares at the strike price.
  • Delta indicates directional exposure and hedging requirements: The net Delta of a call vertical spread is calculated by summing the Deltas of the short and long legs. A non-zero net Delta indicates the position has directional exposure that can be hedged by holding shares of the underlying asset.

Understanding call vertical spreads enables traders to generate income while limiting upside risk in bearish market conditions. ___
